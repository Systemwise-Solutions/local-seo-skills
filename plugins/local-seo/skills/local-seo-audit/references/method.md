# Method: how Mani puts the work in order

Factors are only useful if they tell you what to do first. Two models do that.

## The Visibility Ladder

A business has to clear five rungs, and each depends on the one below. Work on a higher rung is wasted while a lower one is broken. Start at the bottom and stop at the first break. That break is where the plan starts.

| Rung | The question | Typical breaks |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Eligible | Can Google and AI systems confirm the business exists? | Unverified or suspended profile. Site not indexed. Different name, address or phone on the site, profile and main directories. Duplicate listings. |
| 2. Understood | Do they know what it does? | Wrong or too broad primary category. Services missing from the profile. No page per core service. Profile links to a homepage that lists everything. |
| 3. Ranked | Does it appear across the area it serves? | Strong at the door, gone half a mile away. Competitors with better proof take over at the edges. |
| 4. Chosen | Do people pick it over the two businesses beside it? | Few recent reviews. No replies. Stock photos. Missing prices, hours or booking link. |
| 5. Named | Do AI assistants recommend it? | Few other sources describe it. Inconsistent descriptions. Thin site content an assistant can't quote. |

A clinic with the wrong primary category doesn't need a review campaign yet. The rungs overlap in practice. The model earns its place because it stops people fixing the interesting problem before the important one.

## Proximity, Prominence, Proof

Google says local results depend on relevance, distance and prominence. In terms you can act on:

- **Proximity**: you can't move it, but you can measure where you are close enough to compete, and make sure you win there. The useful question is "where am I close enough to compete, and am I winning there?", not "how do I beat proximity?".
- **Prominence**: built slowly. Reviews, links, local mentions, branded searches.
- **Proof**: evidence you do what you say. Primary category, services, one page per service, review text that names the job and the place, other sites describing you the same way.

When a business ranks for its name but not its services, it is nearly always a proof problem. It is also the part the owner controls most directly, which is why most audits find their best fixes there.

## What the factors mean in practice

- **Primary category** is the strongest relevance signal the owner controls. Check it first when they rank for their name but not their main service. Secondary categories help but never make up for the wrong primary.
- **Landing page.** The page the profile links to should say plainly what the business does and where. A clinic linking to a homepage listing 40 treatments is weaker for each treatment than one linking to a page about that treatment. One page per core service, written for the person searching.
- **Reviews.** Past a basic threshold, recency and a steady flow matter more than the total. Review text that mentions the service and the place helps relevance. Replies matter more for winning the click than for ranking. The quickest volume fix is timing: an SMS with a one-tap review link within the hour the job ends, one email follow-up two or three days later, and a QR code at the counter or on the invoice.
- **Consistency.** Mismatched names, old addresses and different phone numbers create doubt for Google and for AI systems. Fixing them rarely causes a jump on its own, but leaving them broken caps everything else.
- **Prominence beyond reviews.** Links from local sites, local press, sponsorships, suppliers and associations. Slow to build and hard to copy, which is why they hold.
- **AI answers** lean on more than the map pack: directories, comparison pages, local press, review text and the business's own site. A business described the same way by several sources looks safer to name. Answers vary from run to run, so a single screenshot proves nothing. Ask the same question several times and count.

## Don't prioritise these

Tell the user plainly when they are about to spend effort here.

- **Showpiece website design.** Most local searchers are on a phone and want to call or book in a few taps. A fast page with the phone number, prices and booking button up front does more than a redesign.
- **Programmatic location pages.** Hundreds of near-identical "service in town" pages. They rarely rank for long, dilute the pages that matter, and give AI assistants nothing to quote. A handful of pages with real local detail beats them.
- **Vanity metrics.** Blog traffic that never turns into calls or bookings. Judge work on enquiries from the areas served.
- **Service area settings.** They describe where you work. They don't widen where you rank. The verified address still anchors proximity, even when hidden.
- **Posting to the profile for rankings.** Useful for people looking at the profile. Not a ranking lever on its own.
- **An average rank.** "Average position 4.2" can mean first at the door and absent a mile away. Report the map, not one number.

## Reading a grid

A geo-grid is the same search run from a grid of points around the business.

- **Strong radius**: the distance from the business within which it ranks in the top 3 at most points. This is the single most useful number in local SEO.
- **Coverage**: share of points where it ranks top 3, and top 10.
- **Edge competitors**: who takes over where the business drops out. Compare their proof (category, services, page, reviews) with the business's.

When the strong radius is smaller than the area customers come from, the fix is proof and prominence: the right primary category, services, a page for the service, review text that names the service and the surrounding areas, and local links. Service area settings won't widen it. If the business is out-proofed by an edge competitor, compare the two side by side and close the gaps in that order.

A small strong radius in a dense city centre can be healthy. The same radius for a roofer covering a county is a problem. Judge it against how far customers travel.
